Layer Stack Method

Playing one part at a time on Move and stacking them in Live — a band of one, overdubbed. Each part is its own Move Set, played against a rendered bed of everything already recorded, so nothing is ever written in silence.

Why this shape

Overdubbing, one player at a time

Playing one instrument line at a time is not a limitation to work around — it is how records get made. A session tracks the drummer, then the bassist plays to the drums, then the keys play to both. Each musician hears everything already recorded in their headphones and plays to it. You are doing the same thing, playing every chair yourself.

Which makes one thing non-negotiable: the headphone mix. Here that is the bed — a rendered loop of every part already committed, sitting on Move's track 1. Tracks 2–4 are the part you are playing today. The four-track ceiling stops being a limit and becomes the session rule: you always hear the band, you can only add one player at a time.

Move what a part Set holds

  • Track 1 — the bed. 8 bars from Live. Your headphone mix. Never edited, never synced back.
  • Tracks 2–4 — today's player. Three tracks covers kick/snare/hats, or bass plus its octave double, or a chord part and its counter-line.

If one player needs more than three tracks, they are two players.

Live what a part becomes

  • One group track per player, named L3 RHYTHM.
  • One instrument inside it — the player's instrument. That is what makes them swappable later without touching the performance.
  • The bed track is deleted on import. It was the headphone feed, not the record.
Agrees with the playbook

The Move to Live Playbook's invariant now reads one Set = one part — one layer of one song, played against a bed. The two documents describe the same rule.

Tracking order · bottom up

The band

Six chairs, tracked in the order a session would track them. Each player hears everyone below and nobody above, so the order is a dependency chain rather than a preference. Read from the bottom.

What playing alone costs you

Band logic

A real band self-regulates by ear: nobody plays over the singer, the bassist hears the kick, everyone stops in the breakdown. Overdubbing alone removes all of that, because when you record a part by itself you naturally fill the space. These are the rules to apply deliberately in its place.

Everybody rests

The single biggest tell of a one-person overdub: every part plays all the time, because each was recorded alone with room to fill.

The rule: after recording a part, delete roughly a quarter of it. Take out the bars where it was only playing because it could. Space is what makes the parts sound like people.

One owner per lane

Two players in the same frequency lane sound like mud, not thickness. Assign the register before you play the part, not at mix time.

L2 bass40–250 Hz
L3 rhythm250 Hz–2 kHz
L4 lead1–5 kHz
L5 atmospherethe gaps

Call and response

Parts should answer each other, not talk simultaneously. When the lead plays, the rhythm part thins out; when the lead rests, the rhythm fills.

Practically: while tracking L4, listen for where L3 is busy — and play in the holes. This is easy to hear against the bed and impossible to hear in isolation.

The rhythm section is one decision

Drums and bass are not two independent parts. Decide their relationship explicitly before tracking L2: bass locks to the kick, or bass deliberately plays around it.

If you cannot say which, the low end will fight itself no matter how you EQ it later.

Take discipline

Play each part three times rather than programming it once. Keep the take with the best feel even if it has flaws, and quantize only the notes that actively fight the grid. Timing imperfection is most of what makes overdubs sound like a band rather than a sequencer — and it is the thing you cannot add back later.

Naming

  • Move Set: Bisonte Club 1 L3 harmony — named on Move, before syncing.
  • Bed: bed_v03.wav — the number is how many layers it contains.
  • Live group: L3 HARMONY.

The bundle name becomes the project name, so getting it right on Move saves a rename later.

Where the generators go

Variations happen in Live after commit, never on Move — Move is for playing, Live is for permuting.

  • euclid 5 16 2 on L1 hats
  • markov 2 8 on L2 bass
  • mutate .15 for L4 variants

You now have Generators by Iftah and MIDI Tools by Philip Meyer installed — the built-in comparison the playbook's open question asked for. Try them before building euclid.

The one technical detail that matters

Rendering the bed

Everything in this method rests on the bed loop being exactly right. A bed that is a few milliseconds long will drift audibly by bar 8 on Move, and you will write a part that locks to a moving target.

SettingValueWhy
Lengthexactly 8 barsLoop start on 1.1.1, end on 9.1.1. Not "roughly the loop".
TailRender Tail: 0 msAny tail adds silence and breaks the loop point.
Rate44.1 kHz · 16-bit WAVMove's native rate. Higher just costs transfer time.
NormalizeOffBed levels must stay comparable across versions.
Tempo on Moveidentical to projectSet it before recording anything into the Set.
Sanity check

Drop the finished bed back into Live on a fresh track and loop it four times against the original. If it stays locked for 32 bars, it is correct.

Things that are always true

Invariants

tempo, key fixed at L1
Decided once, on the foundation Set. Changing tempo later is cheap in Live and impossible on the layer Sets already written.
bed = 8 bars, always
Same length every version, so any layer Set can be opened against any bed.
one Set = one layer
Never write two layers into one Set, even when tracks are free. The Set name is the layer's identity.
track 1 is never yours
It holds the bed. Nothing you compose lives there, so import is always "keep 2–4, drop 1".
audio cycles, Sets don't
Beds and renders travel Live → Move. Sets only travel Move → Live, once.
Standalone to sync
Control Live blocks the file API. Uploading a bed while Move is a controller silently fails.
duplicate before committing
If a layer might keep evolving on the hardware, duplicate the Set on Move before Save As. After that they are separate lineages.
Blocking dependency

The tooling gap

Step 2 has no tool yet

move-sync.py can list, sets and pull — it cannot push. Uploading each bed currently means opening move.local and dragging by hand, once per layer, once per revision. Across six layers that is the friction that kills the method.

The endpoints are already reverse-engineered and the session cookie already exists in .move-cookies.txt. The shape of the call:

# create the song folder once
POST /api/v1/files/Samples/Bisonte/<Song>/

# upload / replace a bed
POST /api/v1/files/Samples/Bisonte/<Song>/bed_v03.wav?overwrite=true
     Content-Type: multipart/form-data

This is Sprint 3 in the playbook. Under the layer method it is Sprint 1 — nothing else in the loop is manual, so this one gap sets the pace of everything.

Concrete shape

First song, six sessions

0

Set up Live

  • Bisonte Template.als — UTIL track with LOM Console, returns, REF track, LUFS meter on master.
  • Six empty group tracks, pre-named L1–L6, pre-coloured to match this document.
  • Renders/seeds/ in the project folder.
  • Write move-sync.py push.
1

L1 the drummer Move

No bed — the one chair tracked blind. Decide tempo and key here and write them down. Kick pattern with at least one off-grid element. Sync, commit to <Song> v01.als, render bed_v01.

2

L2 the bassist Move Live

Bed on track 1, bass on track 2. Decide the kick relationship before you play — lock or play around. Render bed_v02.

3

L3 rhythm + L4 lead Move

Two Sets in one session — fast once the bed carries drums and bass. Rhythm first, render bed_v03, then play the lead into its holes.

4

L5 atmosphere + generators Live

Resample L1–L4 into Granulator III rather than playing something new. First real use of the generative tools: variants per layer, keep at most three, name survivors by function.

5

Arrange, produce, mix Live

Structure from scenes, ear candy written against the arrangement, mix against REF at matched loudness. Render master, stems, and loops for the next song's L1.

The honest risk

Layer-at-a-time favours arrangement clarity over performance energy. A track built this way can sound assembled. The counter is Route B from the playbook — once the stack exists, switch Move to Control Live and jam the whole thing in one take over the arrangement, keeping what the hands did.
